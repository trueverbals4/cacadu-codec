A camera frame in 204 bytes, sent kilometres over LoRaWAN.

Cacadu compresses a 64×64 colour image into a 100-byte learned latent, encrypts it with AES-256-GCM, and sends it as four 51-byte LoRaWAN packets. The receiver rebuilds the image with a matching AI decoder. Machine learning gets a data pipeline that runs on milliwatts and needs no cellular or Wi-Fi link.

01 / THE PROBLEM

ML models are hungry for data from places that have no network.

Most vision and sensing models are trained and run where bandwidth is cheap. The data that matters most often comes from somewhere else: a farm boundary, a water tank, a remote pump station, a production line in a shielded building. Those sites usually have no Wi-Fi, patchy cellular coverage, and no mains power at the sensor.

The usual fixes all cost something. Cellular modems draw hundreds of milliamps and carry a monthly fee per device. Wi-Fi reaches tens of metres. Storing images and collecting them by hand puts days between an event and the model seeing it.

Range

Kilometres, not metres

Sensors sit far from any gateway or access point.

Power

Battery or solar for years

The radio budget has to fit a coin cell or a small panel.

Payload

Images, not just numbers

Vision models need pixels, which low-power links were never built to carry.

The question Cacadu set out to answer: can a useful image fit inside the tiny payload a long-range, low-power radio allows?

02 / LORA + LORAWAN

Long range comes from spreading the signal, and costs payload.

LoRa is the physical radio layer. It uses chirp spread spectrum: each symbol is a tone that sweeps across the whole channel, as in the spectrogram above. A receiver correlates against the known chirp shape, so it can pull a signal out from well below the noise floor. Raising the spreading factor from SF7 to SF12 makes each chirp longer, which buys sensitivity (around −137 dBm at SF12) and range at the cost of data rate.

LoRaWAN is the network protocol on top. End devices broadcast to any gateway in earshot. Gateways forward everything to a network server, which deduplicates, authenticates and routes each frame to an application server. Devices sleep almost all the time and wake only to transmit.

The spread-spectrum advantage

Processing gain lets a LoRa receiver decode signals the noise would bury for a narrowband radio. That gives robust links through foliage, walls and long distances on a few milliwatts, in licence-free ISM bands.

Why ML rarely uses it

51 bytes per uplink and duty-cycle limits (1% in EU868) look incompatible with image data. A raw 64×64 RGB frame is 12,288 bytes, which is 241 maximum-size packets. LoRaWAN has been treated as a link for temperature readings, not for vision.

03 / THE DISCOVERY

The lessy lossy protocol is Cacadu itself.

Cacadu is a learned lossy codec built for the LoRa channel. Squeezing JPEG through a 51-byte payload leaves block artefacts and a header that alone overflows the budget. Cacadu sends PCA latent vectors instead. The encoder learns, from the camera's own images, which directions of variation matter. It keeps the strongest 100 and drops the rest. The decoder rebuilds the scene from those 100 numbers.

The result is lossy by design, but less lossy than a general-purpose format at the same size, because the basis is fitted to the scenes this camera actually sees. The decoded frame is the codec's learned reconstruction rather than a pixel copy. Running it across the full image set exposed two findings.

Why water bottles

The training and test images are water bottles: empty, half-full and full. Bottles were chosen for their high transparency and the way light passes through, bends and reflects off the plastic and water. The background shows through the bottle, and highlights shift with fill level. That makes them a hard case for a codec. The encoder has to learn how light interacts with the scene while keeping the outline, label, cap and fill level intact, which pushes the learning harder than an opaque object would.

Finding 1

Lighting intoxication

SVD-class encoders without mean-centring spend latent capacity on illumination, degrading structure across the whole frame. The loss may be invisible to downstream CNN learning, but the image is structurally incorrect.

With the frame and the byte budget held fixed, the uncentred SVD encoder has 2.3 to 4.8 times the error of the mean-centred PCA encoder on every frame, a gap of 3.7 to 6.8 dB. In the SVD decodes the bottle keeps its overall shape, label band and cap, so a classifier may still read it correctly. But ghost outlines and smeared texture run through the whole frame.

The cause: TruncatedSVD does not subtract the mean image before decomposition, so its leading component is spent on overall brightness. That leaves less of the 100-byte budget for structure. The error spreads across the frame rather than sitting only in the bright pixels: for SVD, error in the brightest quarter of pixels is about the same as in the darkest quarter. Mean-centring removes the brightness offset first, which is why PCA wins everywhere.

Why it matters: a model trained on these images learns from data that looks right but is not. Accuracy metrics may not flag it. Only a structural check against the original shows the error.

Finding 2

The 100-byte optimum

Titrating the latent size across 50, 75, 100, 150 and 200 bytes, in both colour and greyscale, puts the MSE minimum at 100 bytes for this image class and LoRa use case.

The 100-byte size was the starting choice. The sweep tested it rather than assuming it. Below 100 bytes the latent drops detail the decoder can't recover. Above it, error rises again in this pipeline. Components past 100 add almost no signal: explained variance is already 99.6% at 100. They do add rounding noise to the int8 latent, and each frame needs more packets, so more can be lost.

04 / THE ARCHITECTURE

Seven stages from camera to decoded image.

The case study is the Cacadu ImageGenAI pipeline. Each stage is a separate Python module, so the radio simulation in stage 5 can be swapped for an SX1276 or SX1262 driver without touching anything else.

  1. 1

    Capture stage1_capture.py

    Loads camera frames and resizes them to 64×64 RGB. Folder names act as class labels (empty bottles, full bottles, backgrounds and more).

    → 64 × 64 × 3 = 12,288 bytes
  2. 2

    AI encoder stage2_encoder.py

    IncrementalPCA with 100 components, trained in batches of 128. The model can keep learning in the field with partial_fit_batch(). Latents are normalised and quantised to int8.

    → 100 bytes · 122.9 : 1
  3. 3

    Encrypt stage3_encrypt.py

    AES-256-GCM with a fresh 12-byte nonce per frame and a 16-byte authentication tag.

    → 12 + 100 + 16 = 128 bytes
  4. 4

    Packetise + FEC stage4_packetize.py

    Splits the blob into three 48-byte data chunks, adds a 3-byte header to each, and XORs them into one parity packet.

    → 4 packets × 51 bytes
  5. 5

    Transmit stage5_transmit.py

    Simulated LoRa air link with configurable packet drop and bit-flip corruption. On a NACK it resends only the missing chunks.

    → 5% drop, 0.5% corruption in the benchmark run
  6. 6

    Receive stage6_receive.py

    Reassembles the frame, recovers a single loss from parity, rejects replayed frame IDs, and sends a NACK when two or more packets are lost. It then decrypts and verifies the tag.

    → ok · fec_recovered · resent_ok · failed
  7. 7

    AI decoder stage7_decoder.py

    Inverse PCA turns the 100-byte latent back into a 64×64 image: the decoder's learned reading of what the camera saw.

    → 64 × 64 × 3 decoded image

Loss recovery

ok

All three data packets arrive. Deliver at once.

fec_recovered

One data packet lost. XOR parity rebuilds it with no extra airtime.

resent_ok

Two or more lost. The receiver sends a 7-byte NACK (frame ID, missing-chunk bitmask, timestamp) and only those chunks are resent.

failed

The resend also fails, the GCM tag check fails, or the frame ID is a replay. The frame is dropped.

05 / SECURE IOT

Security in layers, from the radio to the image.

LoRaWAN was designed for networks of thousands of unattended devices, so security is part of the protocol. Devices join over the air (OTAA) and derive session keys from a root key that never crosses the air. A network session key signs every frame with a message integrity code, and frame counters block replays. A separate application session key encrypts the payload, so the network operator routes traffic it cannot read.

Cacadu adds its own end-to-end layer on top. Gateways and network servers carry only AES-256-GCM ciphertext. Any change to a packet in transit makes the tag check fail, so a corrupted image is rejected rather than decoded.

Cacadu application layerAES-256-GCM per frame, fresh 12-byte nonce, 16-byte tag. Frame-ID replay rejection. Only the ground station holds the key.
LoRaWAN applicationAppSKey (AES-128) encrypts the payload end to end between device and application server.
LoRaWAN networkNwkSKey message integrity code on every frame, frame counters, OTAA join with device-unique root keys.
GatewayForwards packets it cannot decrypt. A compromised gateway can drop traffic but cannot read or forge it.
LoRa radioChirp spread spectrum is hard to jam narrowly. This is robustness, not secrecy.
Scale

One gateway serves thousands of devices. Adding a sensor means provisioning a key, not running a cable.

Small attack surface

Devices accept almost no inbound traffic. In Cacadu the only downlink is a short NACK.

Production gap

The prototype generates the AES key per session in memory. A deployment needs keys provisioned to a secure element and a key-rotation plan.

On real frames from the bottle-classification set, the mean-centred encoder keeps what the classifier needs: the bottle outline, label band, cap and fill level. Full bottles decode at 37.7 dB, in the "good" band. Empty bottles score lower, 33.8 and 34.8 dB, likely because light passes through the empty plastic and the background shows through, which leaves more fine detail to encode.

Sample frames from the dataset (full bottle 20260913_220309, empty bottle 20260913_221234) with the linear encoder benchmark · 200 images · 100-byte latent · greyscale · 5% LoRa drop. IncrementalPCA leads at 42.6 dB and MSE 3.6.

Over a realistic lossy link, the full colour pipeline sits in the 30–35 dB "acceptable" band that broadcast video uses. Each image costs 204 bytes of airtime instead of 12,288. On greyscale input the same encoder family reaches 42.6 dB, which looks close to lossless.

Across 995 real images at 5% simulated packet drop, no frame was lost. Parity recovered every single-packet loss without a resend. Selective resend recovered every double loss. The GCM tag caught every corrupted packet.

Both findings above are part of the filing. Lighting intoxication is the reason the claims specify a mean-centred encoder. The latent-size sweep is claimed as a method for finding the best latent size for any quantised codec on a fixed packet structure. The application groups its claims as follows.

Inference-gated transmission

An on-device classifier sends a frame to the codec only when it recognises an object of interest above a set confidence. Empty frames never use airtime.

Codec pipeline

A mean-centred learned encoder, AES-256-GCM with the tag checked before decryption, and three data packets plus one XOR parity packet with selective resend.

Latent-size method

Sweeping quantised latent sizes through the radio link to find the quality peak, where higher sizes lose quality to accumulated quantisation noise.

CPU gate before GPU

A fast CPU reconstruction scores each received frame. Only frames that pass go to the GPU decoder. The rest trigger a resend first.

Generative decoder

A convolutional decoder trained with a variational objective, turning the codec into a generative model that works on the same 100-byte latent.

LoRa physical layer and field learning

Transmission written directly to the LoRa radio, with per-packet spreading factor and bandwidth, plus encoder updates from new frames without stopping the pipeline.

Open source

LoRaWAN-ImageGenAI

The seven-stage codec running over the standard LoRaWAN stack, compatible with commercial gateways and network servers.

Proprietary

LoRa-ImageGenAI

The same codec driving the LoRa radio chipset directly, with no LoRaWAN MAC layer, custom packet timing and adaptive modulation.
